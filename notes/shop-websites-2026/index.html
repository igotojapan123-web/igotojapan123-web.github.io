Why I did this

I build websites and I was looking for shops whose sites were costing them work. Search results are useless for that: a shop that ranks on Google has usually looked after its site. So I went to the lists that don’t care about quality — chapter member directories of a national tooling and machining association, plus a few similar lists — collected every website link, and checked each one the way a purchasing engineer would at 6pm with a drawing to send.

I expected “ugly”. Ugly turned out to be the least of it. The things that actually stop a buyer are duller and more specific than that.

The five things that actually stop a buyer

1. There is no way to send you the drawing

A quarter of the sites have no form. On many of them the contact page is a phone number, a fax number and a street address. Some add one email — often a personal one at a cable-company domain. The buyer has to leave your site, open their own mail program, remember which address, and attach the file themselves. Every one of those steps is a place where they decide to try the next shop on the list instead.

One site had a page literally titled Request a Quote. It was a link that opened the visitor’s email program. Another had a Quotes page that said, in full, “Please send us your email RFQs here.”

2. The phone version is broken and nobody knows

Most owners look at their own site on the office PC, where it is fine. On a phone, 20 of the 136 sites I opened render the full desktop layout shrunk to fit — 980 to 1,130 pixels squeezed into 390. The text ends up at roughly a third of readable size.

The worst case was invisible from a desktop: one site detects a phone, redirects it to a separate mobile address, and that mobile address has been a “404 Not Found” page for years. Every single phone visitor gets an error page. The desktop site is fine, so nobody noticed.

3. “Not secure” next to your name

Chrome now tries the secure (https) version of a site first. On 11% of these sites you land on plain http, and on 2% the secure version does not exist at all — the server returns a protocol error. The address bar then says Not secure beside the company name. Buyers at larger companies often cannot even open those pages; the corporate laptop blocks them.

A nastier variant: the secure version exists but is broken. On one site the https version loads with no menu styling and a black block where the photo slider should be, because four stylesheets and two scripts are still pulled over plain http and the browser blocks them. The http version looks fine. Which one a visitor gets is a coin toss.

4. Widgets for services that no longer exist

Google+ buttons (Google+ closed in 2019) on at least three sites, throwing a blocked-content error on every page load. A page built with Adobe Spry widgets, which Adobe abandoned in 2012. A footer that reads “This template is made with Colorlib” directly under an ISO 9001:2015 section. And one page whose code still checks whether the visitor is running Netscape Navigator 4.

None of these break the site. All of them are read by a careful buyer as “nobody has touched this in a decade”, which is exactly the opposite of what the ISO section above it is trying to say.

5. Facts that contradict each other

The one that stuck with me: a header badge saying ISO 9001:2015, and three screens down a paragraph saying “we are an ISO 9001:2008 company”. The 2008 standard was withdrawn in 2018. The certificate on the wall is almost certainly the 2015 one — the paragraph just never got updated. But a quality auditor at a customer reads that paragraph.

What the good ones have in common

It is not design. Some of the best sites in the set are plain. They have: the words a buyer searches for on the home page (materials, processes, tolerances, certifications, industries), a form that accepts a file, photographs of their own floor and their own parts rather than stock images, and https that just works. That is the whole list. A shop that has been going since 1941 with a 20,000 square foot floor and three photos on its website is underselling itself far more than a shop with an ugly logo.

Method and limits

  • Source. 337 website links collected from public member directories of a US tooling-and-machining association’s regional chapters, plus member lists of two martial-arts federations and one metalforming association. Large companies (a bank, a tool distributor, an arms maker) and the associations themselves were removed before counting.
  • Reachable. 283 returned a normal page. 54 were dead domains, refused connections, timed out, or returned an error page. Some of those may block automated requests rather than being truly dead, so treat 16% as an upper bound.
  • Static checks (https behaviour, form presence on the home page and up to five contact-like pages, email addresses, copyright year, viewport tag) were run against the page source of all 283.
  • Browser checks (phone rendering, mixed-content errors, broken images, what happens on https vs http) were run in real Chrome on a desktop profile and an iPhone profile, but only for the 136 sites that showed at least one warning sign in the static pass. The “1 in 7 render at desktop width” figure is therefore of that subset, not of all 283 — the true share across all sites is lower.
  • Forms. “No form” means no <form> element on the home page or on the contact pages I could find. A site might have a form on a page I didn’t reach. A few sites load forms with JavaScript that a static pass can’t see; the browser pass caught some of those.
  • No names. I have written to some of these shops privately. I’m not naming any of them here, and the examples above are described, not linked.
