The free class nobody can claim

Almost every school site leads with the same offer: Your first class is free. It is the right offer. Then I tried to claim it the way a parent would — from a phone, at night, without calling anyone. On a third of the sites there is nothing to fill in. The contact page is a phone number, an address and, if you are lucky, a Gmail address. The parent has to remember to call tomorrow during work hours. The school never finds out how many didn’t.

This is the single most expensive gap on a school website, and it is also the cheapest to fix: one form — parent’s name, child’s age, preferred day — that lands in the instructor’s inbox with a confirmation to the parent.

The other things a parent notices

A Gmail address on the school’s own website

Ten of the 33 schools list only a free email address. It works, and it isn’t a mark of a bad school — but on a page that also says “since 1986” and “ITF certified”, it reads as a smaller operation than it is. An address at your own domain is included with almost every hosting plan and takes an afternoon to set up.

Sites built and forgotten

One site loads its core script over plain http from a 2000s site-builder, using a method browsers have been warning about for years. One asks for its heading font over http on an https page, so the browser blocks it and every visitor sees the fallback font — the site has looked “slightly off” for years without anyone being able to say why. Footers reading 2019 and 2021. None of this breaks the page. All of it dates it, and parents notice dates.

What the good ones do

The best sites in the set are not the prettiest. They have a schedule that reads on a phone, the instructor’s name and lineage, a real photo of the training floor rather than a stock image, the free-class form at the top, and a secure address. That is the entire list.

After the form: the part most school sites never get to

A form gets the child through the door. What keeps a family for years is what happens next, and almost none of it lives on the website: attendance, belt progress, the notice about Saturday’s testing, the video of the pattern to practise at home, the instructor’s note to the parent. In most schools that runs through a group chat — and the message from March about testing fees is forty screens up.

That is what a member system is for: students and parents log in, see today’s class and their own attendance, watch the assigned material, submit a video, receive feedback, and read notices the school can see they have opened. I build these for academies; you can open two of them from the work page. It is a separate project from the website, and not every school needs one on day one — but it is the reason a website for a school should be built with the door left open for it.

Method and limits

  • Source. 42 school links from the public member lists of ITF America, a Tang Soo Do association and a Korean-martial-arts association; 33 returned a normal page.
  • Checks. Page source of all 33 for https behaviour, forms on the home page and up to five contact-like pages, email addresses, and copyright year; a real-Chrome pass (desktop and iPhone profile, console errors) for the ones with a warning sign.
  • Sample size. 33 is small. I’m publishing it because the direction matches the 283-site manufacturer set and because school owners asked. Treat percentages as rough.
